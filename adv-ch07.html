Sampling & the sample mean

We rarely see a whole population — we work with samples. A sample's mean estimates the population mean, but it wobbles from sample to sample. Larger samples wobble less.

In [ ]: python3
TIP This is the intuition behind why more data helps: larger samples give estimates that sit closer to the truth, more reliably. It's also why a model trained on tiny data is risky.

Lesson

Distributions

A distribution describes how values are spread. The normal (bell curve) is the most common in statistics. Knowing a variable's distribution guides which methods and models are appropriate.

In [ ]: python3

Lesson

Hypothesis testing & p-values

A hypothesis test asks: could this result be due to chance? You compare two groups and get a p-value — the probability of seeing a difference this big if there were truly no difference. Small p (by convention < 0.05) suggests a real effect.

In [ ]: python3
READ THIS CAREFULLY A p-value is not the probability your hypothesis is true, and 'not significant' doesn't mean 'no effect' — it means 'not enough evidence here'. Misreading p-values is one of the most common mistakes in data science.

Lesson

Confidence intervals

A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for an estimate, instead of a single number. A 95% CI means: if you repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true value.

In [ ]: python3

Lesson

Why this matters for ML

Statistics is how you tell signal from noise. It tells you whether a feature really relates to the target, whether one model truly beats another, and how much to trust a number. Models make predictions; statistics tells you whether to believe them.

EX 1

Fraction within one std

For a standard normal sample, the fraction within 1 std of the mean is about 0.68. Using the seeded sample below, print that fraction rounded to 2 decimals.

In [ ]: python3
Show solution

import numpy as np np.random.seed(1) s = np.random.normal(0, 1, 10000) print(round(float(np.mean(np.abs(s) < 1)), 2))
